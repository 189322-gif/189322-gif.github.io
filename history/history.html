A short history of the drum set

The drum set is a flexible instrument assembled from drums, cymbals and pedals so one player can shape a whole rhythm section. Its story is one of changing music, changing technology and creative players finding new ways to keep time.

Before the kitTimelineListen through historySources

Before one player sat behind the kit

People have used struck instruments for dancing, communication and ceremony for a very long time. Much later, marching bands and theatre ensembles brought snare drums, bass drums and cymbals into organised performance. The modern drum set emerged when these separate voices were arranged so that one person could play them together. Yamaha places this change near the end of the nineteenth century and credits the foot-operated bass drum as a crucial step.1

Hands and feet

A bass-drum pedal let a player combine a low beat from the foot with snare and cymbal sounds from the hands. The kit became a coordinated instrument rather than a row of separate percussion parts.

An instrument that changes

There has never been only one standard setup. Players add, remove and rearrange drums, cymbals and other percussion to fit the music they play.2

Sound and space

The history of drumming is also a history of choices: which voice keeps time, which accent answers the band and when silence is more effective than another note.

Milestones in the kit's development

Late 1800s to early 1900s

The combined kit takes shape

Snare, bass drum and cymbals began to be gathered for a single performer. Yamaha describes New Orleans drummer Dee Dee Chandler as an early figure associated with using a bass-drum pedal while playing snare. The precise history involved many players and makers, but the pedal made the one-person kit practical.1

Early jazz

New Orleans players expand the vocabulary

Early jazz drummers combined street-band traditions with dance music. Warren “Baby” Dodds used changing colours from snare, cymbals and other percussion; the Percussive Arts Society describes his use of a cymbal as a timekeeping voice and his influence on later jazz drumming.3

Swing era

Time moves around the kit

Jo Jones helped make the hi-hat a flowing home for the jazz pulse, closing it on beats two and four. As players explored lighter bass-drum sounds and more active cymbal parts, the kit gained new shades of motion and space.4

1930s and beyond

Toms and cymbal hardware evolve

Gene Krupa worked with drum makers on tunable toms, helping broaden the melodic possibilities of a kit. Later improvements in heads, stands and hardware made setups more stable and easier to adapt to different performance settings.56

Bebop and modern jazz

The ride cymbal becomes a centre of gravity

Max Roach was among the drummers who brought the ride cymbal to the foreground and used the rest of the kit for conversation with the ensemble. This approach gave the drummer more freedom to shape a phrase while still keeping time.7

Rock to the present

New styles, new setups

As amplified music grew, players sought different cymbal sounds, stronger hardware and broader dynamic range. Today an acoustic kit may be compact or expansive, and electronic sounds can join the setup too. The instrument continues to change with the music.26
